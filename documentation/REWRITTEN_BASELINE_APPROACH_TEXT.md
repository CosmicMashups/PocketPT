# Rewritten Baseline Approach Text

The initial baseline approach employed a rule-based system that assessed range of motion by comparing computed joint angles against predefined acceptable ranges derived from clinical protocols and physical therapist expertise. This approach represented the simplest plausible automation strategy, translating explicit heuristics that therapists articulate when describing acceptable range of motion into deterministic algorithmic rules. For each assessment type, clinical guidelines specified target angle ranges for relevant joints at key points in the movement trajectory.

The rule-based baseline computed joint angles from detected keypoint coordinates using geometric formulas, then compared observed angles against predefined thresholds using simple conditional logic. If all relevant angles fell within acceptable ranges and additional constraints on movement symmetry and trunk stability were satisfied, the range of motion measurement was classified as within normal limits. If any angle violated its threshold or if compensatory patterns were detected, the measurement was flagged as indicating restricted range of motion. This approach required no machine learning training phase, instead relying entirely on domain expertise encoded as explicit rules.

The advantage of this baseline lay in its interpretability and clinical alignment, as the decision logic directly reflected verbal descriptions therapists provide when assessing range of motion. Each flagged restriction could be traced to a specific violated constraint, providing transparent explanations for assessment decisions. However, the baseline exhibited fundamental limitations in handling natural variability and ambiguity inherent in human movement. Real range of motion measurements rarely matched idealized angle specifications exactly, as individual anatomical differences, flexibility variations, and minor movement fluctuations caused continuous variation around target values.

Empirical evaluation of the rule-based baseline on the validation dataset yielded overall accuracy of sixty-two point three percent, with precision of fifty-eight point one percent and recall of seventy-one point two percent. This performance established a meaningful lower bound demonstrating that even simple automation could provide some value, but also clearly indicated substantial room for improvement through more sophisticated approaches. The relatively low precision revealed frequent false alarms where the system incorrectly flagged normal range of motion as restricted, often due to individual variations falling outside rigid threshold boundaries. The higher recall indicated better success at identifying genuinely restricted range of motion, though still missing approximately twenty-nine percent of actual restrictions. These baseline results provided a critical reference point against which to evaluate the improvements achieved through machine learning approaches.
